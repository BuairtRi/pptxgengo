# West Monroe presentation content and narrative

Write for an executive or delivery audience that needs to make a decision, assess value, understand a proposed approach, or align on action. Follow the brief's audience, purpose, stage, and requested outcome; do not bake a client, pursuit, or sample deck into the skill.

## Shape the argument

For each slide, identify its role in the story and state the point in an assertion title. Give the reader a clear takeaway, then support it with the evidence and relationships needed to understand it. A list of topics is not a narrative. Connect current conditions or opportunity to an implication, the proposed response, measurable outcomes, and the decision or next step when relevant.

Use a coherent sequence: orient the audience, establish why action matters, show what is proposed and how it works, explain value and delivery, then make the requested decision/action explicit. Adjust this to the source material; do not force every deck into a fixed slide count or boilerplate outline.

## Evidence and claims

Keep verified facts separate from hypotheses, unverified statements, estimates, and synthetic examples. Attribute external or client-provided facts to their source and preserve qualifications. Quotes must be exact and traceable to a supplied source. Label fictional people, allocations, metrics, and scenarios as illustrative. Do not convert a target into a commitment or a potential benefit into a guaranteed result.

Preserve the detail that makes a proposal actionable: mechanisms, scope boundaries, roles/ownership, dependencies, acceptance conditions, evidence, assumptions, and decision gates. If the available content is incomplete, surface the assumption or open question instead of silently filling it with invented specifics.

## Concision and density

Use concise language, but retain substance. Remove repetition and generic promotional claims before cutting proof, qualifications, or delivery detail. Use parallel phrasing for comparable activities and outcomes. Spell out acronyms on first use when the audience may not know them. Use numbers with units, period, and basis; distinguish baselines, targets, and illustrative values.

Detailed proposals may need dense slides. Match information density and visual structure to the reference content's relationships. Do not solve overflow by deleting required meaning or shrinking typography until it is unreadable. Split an argument or choose a structure with adequate capacity. Verify the text at its final rendered size.

## West Monroe voice

Use direct, specific, collaborative language. Sound confident through concrete evidence and clear reasoning, not superlatives or certainty the evidence cannot support. Make client outcomes and practical actions visible. Avoid generic slogans, vague transformation language, unexplained jargon, and claims of unique superiority unless the supplied evidence supports them.
