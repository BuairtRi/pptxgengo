# West Monroe brand voice

Every page of a West Monroe deck is written in this voice. Ri's personal voice ([ri-slide-voice.md](ri-slide-voice.md)) applies on top of it by default. Read both before writing slide copy.

## The idea behind the voice

> Other consultancies talk at you. At West Monroe, we work with you.

Write like the person a client calls when they really need something done: the one who knows how to do it, dives in, and brings the client's team along. The brand calls this **intelligence in action**: honest, clear and direct, with the confidence of people who know what they are doing and enjoy doing it well. "More walk. Less talk."

Three traits shape the tone:

| Trait | On a slide, it sounds like | It does not sound like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passionately pragmatic | The first thing we would do, who does it, what changes | Transformation language with no first step |
| Kindred creators | "We" and "you" on the same problem; the client team's role is visible | "Our proprietary methodology delivers…" |
| Fiercely energized | Strong verbs, real numbers, a dated next step | Exclamation marks, superlatives, "exciting journey" |

Energy comes from substance: a sharp observation about the reader's situation, a concrete plan, a number that matters. It does not come from adjectives.

## The four building blocks, applied to slides

### 1. Start with the point

Decide what the reader must take away, then put it first. The slide title states it as a sentence. The first line of body copy supports it directly; no scene-setting.

- "Say what you mean, don't tell me what it means." Do not write copy that describes its own importance ("a critical insight") or interprets instead of stating ("represents a significant opportunity").
- Simple words create more trust: "use" not "leverage", "start" not "commence".
- If you cannot state the page's point in one sentence, the page is not ready to write.

Before: "Leveraging advanced analytics to unlock supply chain visibility"
After: "Your planners can see stockouts four days earlier if we connect the warehouse feed to the forecast"

### 2. Remember we're bilingual

We are fluent in industry and technology. Show it by connecting each technical element to what a person or the business can now do.

- Pair technology with its effect in the same bullet, table row or diagram label: "a shared data layer, so finance and operations close the month on the same numbers."
- Demonstrate knowledge with specifics only a practitioner would know: where this kind of project usually stalls, the system that always causes trouble.
- Bring a point of view. A page that lists options without recommending one is a catalog.
- Use the audience's technical terms precisely when they use them; explain them when they do not. Precision is not jargon. Jargon is a word used to sound expert.

Before: "AI-powered intelligent document processing platform"
After: "Extract policy data from scanned forms so underwriters stop retyping it"

### 3. Use "we" language

Speak with people, not at them: the same table, not a podium. Reassure the reader that they will not have to solve this alone.

- "We" for genuinely shared work, "you" and "your team" for the reader. Never "the client", "the organization" or "resources" on a client-facing page.
- "We" does not blur ownership. Where responsibility matters, name it: "Your product owners set priorities each sprint; our team runs the build and testing."
- Show the client's part in the approach: who joins working sessions, who decides, what their team learns.
- Invite participation with a specific ask: a decision, a session, access to a system, a person to meet.

Before: "The consultant will conduct stakeholder interviews and deliver a current-state assessment."
After: "We'll interview your plant managers in week one and walk your team through what we find before we recommend anything."

### 4. Generate momentum

Quick, precise language that moves. Value shown through specifics and real stories.

- Active voice with a named actor. Strong verbs (cut, replace, launch, consolidate, retire) over weak ones (enable, facilitate, support).
- Specifics over generalizations: "three weeks", "two of your four regions".
- A real example from a similar engagement proves more than a claim of expertise. Use only examples supplied in the sources or by the operator; never invent one.
- Each page moves the argument forward. The deck ends with a step the reader can take, with a date or an owner.

Before: "Next steps will be determined following further discussion of priorities."
After: "Pick two plants for the pilot by May 15 so we can start the baseline in June."

## Instead of / Try this

The brand's own paired examples show the pattern: turn the focus to the reader, replace an abstract claim with an observable one, lead with the fact that differentiates.

| Instead of | Try this |
| --- | --- |
| Where forward thinking meets practical application. | Let's make smart thinking work for you |
| …partnering with companies in transformative industries to deliver quantifiable financial value. We believe that digital is a mindset... | If you're transforming your industry, you need a partner who works smarter to solve sooner and grow tangible value… |
| Learn everything West Monroe has to offer, from a supportive, collaborative culture to teammates who grow with you… | People come back to West Monroe. Why? Because when you put people first, supporting each other and co-creating a rewarding work environment is the natural outcome… |

Why the second column works: more personal, direct, collaborative and practical; turns the focus on the reader; conversational; differentiates with a fact.

## Approved messaging

| Material | Use |
| --- | --- |
| "At West Monroe, we work with you." | Cover, opening or closing page, as written. Not a slogan on content pages. |
| "Clear Action. Lasting Impact." | Official logo/tagline artwork only, on covers or closings. |
| "Own the now. Win the future." | Section openers or closings when it fits the argument. |
| Elevator pitch | A starting point for an introduction page; adapt it to the audience. |
| Boilerplate | Only where a formal company description is required (RFP "about the firm", "About West Monroe"). Use it as written; do not paraphrase its recognition claims or reuse them as client-specific proof. Ask the operator to confirm the facts are current. |

Elevator pitch: "At West Monroe, we work with you. We're a global consulting firm fluent in business and technology, building practical solutions with lasting impact. We work with clients to solve their most pressing issues to drive value through technology."

Boilerplate: "West Monroe is an AI-native global consulting firm built for today's pace of change. We combine deep industry expertise with modern technology and business transformation capabilities to help organizations move faster and deliver measurable impact with speed to value and certainty of results through execution excellence. We don't just talk about what's possible: we apply AI and technology where it matters most, pairing strategic insights with hands-on execution to help clients move from strategy to results, faster. Clients tell us we show up differently. Our employee ownership model fuels an all-in approach grounded in practical execution, measurable impact, and lasting partnership. That commitment has earned recognition from Forbes and Business Insider and made West Monroe one of only 22 companies recognized worldwide for building high-quality careers."

## Words

**Prefer:** plain verbs; the client's own system names, roles, metrics and industry terms; quantities with units and a basis; "we", "you", "your team".

**Avoid, and say what the thing does instead:**

- Impressive-sounding filler: leverage, synergy, utilize, holistic, robust, seamless, best-in-class, world-class, cutting-edge, next-generation.
- Value gestures with no object: unlock, empower, enable, drive.
- Grand generalizations as standalone ideas: transformation, journey, ecosystem, paradigm, digital mindset.
- Claims every firm makes: deep expertise, trusted partner, commitment to your success, relentless focus on outcomes, uniquely positioned.
- Throat-clearing: "It is important to note", "In today's rapidly changing landscape".
- Passive constructions that hide the actor.

Use American English and the Oxford comma. Avoid em dashes in visible copy; use a colon, a period or a line break.

## Flex by situation

| Situation | Lean toward | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Executive proposal or pursuit | The point first; a clear recommendation; proof on every differentiator | Differentiators a competitor could claim |
| Technical readers | Exact terms and mechanisms | Friendly glosses over technical facts; buzzwords |
| Discovery or working session | "We" language, questions, invitations | Concluding before the client has contributed |
| Status, risk or bad news | The issue first; what we are doing about it, with a date | Burying the issue; reassurance about effort |
| Internal WM deck | Candor about risk and win logic | Internal win logic leaking into client pages |
