# Narrative and slide copy

Write every page in the [West Monroe brand voice](brand-voice.md) and [Ri's slide voice](ri-slide-voice.md). Read both before drafting copy. This reference covers how to build the argument and write each part of a slide.

## Build the argument before the copy

### 1. Write the narrative

Write a short audience-facing paragraph that states the case the deck will make: the reader's situation, what we think it means, what we propose, and what we are asking them to do. If you cannot write this paragraph, go back to [intake and framing](intake-and-framing.md).

For a pursuit or proposal, a reliable arc is:

1. **Their situation.** What is happening and what it costs them, in their terms.
2. **The insight.** What we see that they may not, and why it matters now.
3. **The stakes.** What happens if nothing changes.
4. **The response.** What we propose and how it works.
5. **Why West Monroe.** Specific, contestable differentiators.
6. **Proof.** Comparable work, numbers, people.
7. **The next step.** The decision or action we are asking for.

Adapt the arc to the deck's job. A perspective piece may stop at the insight and an invitation to talk. A findings readout leads with what we found. A status deck leads with the news.

### 2. Write the outline

List pages in order. For each page give a stable page ID, the title (a full sentence), the planned support (evidence, mechanism or visual), and how it connects to the next page.

Read the titles alone, in order. They should tell the whole story. If they do not, fix the outline before writing body copy.

For proposals, check coverage against the deck's job: situation, outcomes, response, workstreams, deliverables, value, proof, team, timing, assumptions, risks and next steps. Not every deck needs all of them. Missing ones should be a deliberate choice.

Get the operator's decision on the outline before writing full copy, unless they have already said to proceed. Then follow [review packets](review-packets.md) for an independent outline review.

## Write each page

Each page answers one question the reader has. The title gives the answer; the body shows why it is true or how it works; the visual makes the relationship easy to see.

### Slide elements

| Element | Write it as |
| --- | --- |
| **Title** | A sentence-case full sentence stating the page's conclusion. One line where possible, two at most. Reading only the titles should convey the deck. |
| **Eyebrow / section label** | A short literal label: "Approach", "Weeks 1–4", "What we heard". Not a slogan. |
| **Subtitle / lead-in** | Optional. One qualification or consequence the title cannot carry. Never a restatement of the title. |
| **Bullets** | Usually a 2–4 word **bolded lead-in** followed by one sentence. Never more than two sentences. Parallel in structure. |
| **Numbered items** | Only for a real sequence, decisions or asks. |
| **Card or column titles** | Specific nouns or short verb phrases. If the same three titles could appear in any proposal, rewrite them. |
| **Text blocks** | Two or three sentences at most. Make the logic explicit with "because", "so", "if… then". |
| **Table headers and cells** | Headers name the comparison ("Control", "How it works", "What it lets you decide"). Cells are short and parallel. |
| **Diagram labels** | Say what moves, who decides, or what condition advances the flow. Label arrows when their meaning is not obvious. |
| **Big numbers / callouts** | One number with its unit, basis, period and source. Mark estimates and targets as such. |
| **Source line** | Plain and complete. Note illustrative values. |
| **Closing / next steps** | Numbered, each with an owner and a date where known. |

### Fitting copy to the page

Slides need compressed copy. Compress sentences; keep the reasoning.

When copy does not fit:

1. Cut repetition, throat-clearing and self-description first.
2. Turn a paragraph into bolded-lead bullets or a table.
3. Move the relationship into a diagram or chart and keep only the labels in text.
4. Put fewer ideas on the page, or split the idea across two pages, each with its own conclusion title.
5. Move optional depth to a referenced appendix.

Never solve overflow by removing a material qualification, forcing unequal ideas into equal boxes, or shrinking the type until it is hard to read. If a fixed template would force one of those, choose a different template or composition (see [template selection](template-selection.md)).

### Reading mode

For an emailed deck, everything the reader needs must be visible on the page; speaker notes will not be read. A presented deck can carry less on the page, but the titles must still tell the story on their own.

## Evidence and claims

- Keep verified facts, inferences, hypotheses, estimates and illustrative examples separate. Mark illustrative people, metrics and scenarios as illustrative on the page.
- Attribute client-provided and external facts to their source. Quote only exact, traceable text.
- Do not turn a target into a commitment or a potential benefit into a promise.
- Give numbers their unit, period and basis. Distinguish baseline, target and illustrative values.
- Keep the details that make a proposal actionable: mechanisms, scope boundaries, owners, dependencies, acceptance conditions, assumptions, decision gates.
- When proof is missing, write the specific claim with a visible placeholder and add it to the open questions. Do not replace it with a generic claim.

Track each material claim in the claim ledger (see [project templates](project-templates.md)): the claim, its type, sources and locations, basis and units, the visible qualification, and who validates it.

## Internal brief vs. visible copy

Keep two things separate for each page:

- **Internal brief:** the page's job, sources, required content, open issues, private rationale.
- **Visible copy:** exactly what will appear on the slide, including visual labels.

Independent reviewers get only the visible copy. If a page only makes sense with the brief, the page needs more visible explanation.

## Self-check before content review

Run this over every page before sending content for review:

1. **Titles.** Is each title a full sentence stating a conclusion? Do the titles alone tell the story?
2. **Competitor test.** Could another firm sign any sentence unchanged? Rewrite it with a specific fact, mechanism or behavior.
3. **Empty compression.** Does any short line lack an actor, a mechanism, a number or the reader's situation? Rewrite or cut it.
4. **Buzzwords.** Remove leverage, unlock, enable, seamless, robust, holistic, transformation-as-an-idea and similar words (full list in [brand voice](brand-voice.md)).
5. **Reader focus.** Is the page about the reader's situation and outcome more than about us? "You" and "your team" where natural?
6. **Logic.** Are causes and conditions explicit? Is the key term repeated consistently? Is every "this" or "it" clear?
7. **Hedges.** At most one per claim, none stacked, no repeated hedge phrase across the deck.
8. **Proof.** Does each differentiator or benefit carry a specific, sourced proof point or a visible placeholder?
9. **Objection.** Is there a concern the reader is likely to have that the deck should name and answer?
10. **Structure.** Bolded-lead bullets of at most two sentences? Numbers only for sequence or decisions? Would a table or diagram show this more clearly?
11. **Momentum.** Active voice, strong verbs, and a forward-pointing close with owners and dates.
12. **Invention.** Is every anecdote, client name, quote and number from a source or the operator?
