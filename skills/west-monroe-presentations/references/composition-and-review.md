# Component composition and visual review

Use this reference when authoring new slides from components, assembling dense proposal material, selecting packaged visuals, or reviewing output.

## Choose structure by content relationship

Map each slide's assertion and evidence to a visual relationship before choosing components. Examples include sequential phases, parallel workstreams, current/future comparisons, metric-and-proof groupings, role/ownership structures, or a roadmap with decision gates. Use the smallest structure that makes those relationships clear. Preserve distinctions between outputs, outcomes, responsibilities, dependencies, and timing.

The packaged composition capabilities include some combinations of semantic cards, pods/teams, measured panels/grids, canvas elements, diagrams, pinned images, and accents. Exact schemas and maturity vary by route. Use only components surfaced by the packaged library/help and honor their declared limits. Native shapes/text are editable, but they do not automatically gain semantic relationships when a user manually drags them.

## Density and slide design

Use enough space and hierarchy for the reader to scan the main point first and details second. A dense proposal page should preserve meaningful comparison, sequence, ownership, or acceptance information, rather than flattening everything into short bullets. Use a compact table or matrix only when the relationships genuinely compare like with like; otherwise use panels, timelines, or separated statements.

Do not copy a source layout's example text and assume the real content will fit. For fixed source templates, treat geometry and count as fixed. For dynamic composition, use the component's explicit geometry and native measurement evidence. Check bounds, font substitutions, wrapping, contrast, overlaps, retained artwork, and page-level balance.

## Assets and accents

Use packaged West Monroe logos, photography, icons, and illustration only when the package catalog makes the asset available. Preserve image identity and use constraints; pin local asset bytes/hashes when the compose schema requires it. A filename alone is not asset provenance. Match crop, placement, contrast, and surrounding copy to the specific slide.

Accents are supporting emphasis. Select the exact phrase based on the argument and verify placement against native phrase bounds. Keep highlights behind text and underlines aligned to the target. Existing accent geometry examples cover specific artwork/modes/phrases; they do not prove arbitrary images, multi-line phrases, or all rotations can be placed automatically. When the tool reports ambiguity or unsupported geometry, leave a deliberate clear area for manual placement and disclose the remaining step.

## Final review

Render the actual output in Microsoft PowerPoint or the packaged native rendering workflow. Review every slide at presentation size, including opening and closing pages, repeated components, image crops, and any fixed-template retained material. Check:

- The title states the conclusion, and the slide earns that conclusion with evidence.
- Text is readable, fits its intended area, and has clear hierarchy.
- Density preserves required detail without accidental crowding or empty space.
- Labels, dates, ownership, measures, units, and qualifications are consistent.
- Colors and accent marks support meaning and maintain contrast.
- Images, chart material, and inherited source content are relevant and factually safe.
- The story flows across slides, with explicit transitions or decisions where needed.

Record remaining fit, factual, or visual uncertainty plainly. Build success, schema validation, native measurements, and geometry replay are different types of evidence; none alone is a complete design approval.
