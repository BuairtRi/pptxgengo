# Ri's slide voice

Apply this voice by default to every deck, together with the [West Monroe brand voice](brand-voice.md). The operator does not need to ask for it. The goal is a draft Ri recognizes as Ri's own writing and can edit lightly, not a perfect imitation. Ri adds humor during editing; do not write jokes.

The voice is **personal and direct**. It never sounds like marketing. Copy reads as if a capable practitioner who has done this work is explaining it to the reader across a table: specific about what happens, who does it, and why it matters to them.

## The governing rule: every line does work

Ri's first test of any sentence is whether it carries content. It is a test of function, not length.

- Cut lines that are **too clever**: "Steering is the work." It sounds final and says nothing.
- Cut lines that are **only setup**: "Steering is the challenge." followed by the sentence that actually explains it. Keep the explaining sentence.
- Cut **self-description**: "We are fully committed to your success." "We want to be transparent about where things stand."

Slides force compression, and compression is fine. A compressed line passes if it still names **an actor, a mechanism, a number or the reader's situation**. It fails if it trades something concrete for something abstract or exists for rhythm.

| Passes (compressed, concrete) | Fails (compressed, empty) |
| --- | --- |
| "**Two worker pools.** Heavy runs binary-dependent jobs; light runs everything else." | "Get it wrong and you starve the pool that matters." |
| "Brokers submit once; underwriters stop re-keying." | "One intake. Zero friction." |
| "Three intake errors cause 60% of rework." | "Rework is an intake problem." |

When copy does not fit, **put fewer ideas on the slide or break the idea into smaller chunks** (bolded-lead bullets, a table, a diagram, a second slide). Shorten sentences; never remove the reasoning that makes the point understandable.

## Personal and direct, not marketing

| Personal and direct | Marketing |
| --- | --- |
| "Your adjusters re-enter the same claim data in three systems." | "Fragmented systems create inefficiencies across the enterprise." |
| "We'd start with the two plants that miss the most shipments." | "A phased approach to unlock value at scale." |
| "This will be slower for the first two sprints. After that, the team stops re-testing by hand." | "Accelerate delivery with next-generation automation." |
| "Your team keeps the vendor relationship; we take the day-to-day integration work." | "A trusted partnership model that empowers your organization." |

What makes the left column personal: it speaks to "you" and "your team", names real roles and systems, admits a cost where there is one, and states what changes in the reader's week.

## Titles

- **Sentence case, and a full sentence.** If someone read only the titles, they should understand the whole deck. Test this by reading the titles in order before writing any body copy.
- The title states the page's conclusion: "Undocumented stored procedures, not data volume, will set the migration timeline." Not a topic: "Migration considerations."
- Causal and conditional titles are good: "If finance and operations share one data layer, month-end close drops from eight days to five."
- Aim for one line; two is acceptable. A title that needs three lines is carrying body copy.

## Sentences

- **Make the logic explicit.** Use "because", "so", "if… then", "instead of", "on the other hand". Ri prefers a clause that explains another clause to two fragments placed side by side and left for the reader to connect.
- **Repeat the key term.** If the page is about "intake errors", say "intake errors" each time. Do not vary it to "submission issues" and "front-end defects" for elegance; the reader will think these are different things.
- **Name the referent.** Avoid a bare "this" or "it" when the reader could wonder what it refers to. "This approach" or "the triage rule" is clearer.
- **Second person.** Write to the reader: "you", "your team" (never "your people" or "resources"). Avoid "organizations face…" and other impersonal framings.
- **Hedge the inference, assert the principle.** Mark a genuine judgment once: "We think", "likely", "in our experience". State principles plainly. Never stack hedges ("we might want to consider whether…"), and vary the wording when you hedge more than once in a deck.
- **Precise technical terms.** Use the term of art and explain it if needed. Do not swap a precise fact for a friendly gloss: "GitLab is self-hosted and runs our CI/CD pipelines", not "GitLab is where the code actually runs".
- **Numbers with intervals and dates.** "Two to three times as long", "by March 14", "since 2024". Not "significantly", "soon", "in recent years".
- **"Not X, but Y" only when X is a belief the reader actually holds.** "We don't just advise; we work alongside your team" works because clients often assume consultants only advise. "Not just a tool, but a philosophy" fails because nobody believed it was just a tool, and Y is vaguer than X.
- **Name who decided.** When a constraint or decision belongs to someone, attribute it: "Your CFO set the June deadline", not "The deadline is June".

## Persuasion and proof

- **The competitor test.** Could another consulting firm put its name under this sentence unchanged? If yes, the sentence does no work. This replaces any banned-phrase list: it removes "commitment to your success", "relentless focus on outcomes" and "uniquely positioned" on its own.
- **Claims carry their proof, matched to the reader.** A count, the reader's own industry segment and, when approved, a named client: "[N] implementations for regional health plans since [year], including [client]." Not "a proven track record with organizations like yours."
- **A differentiator must be contestable.** It should be something a competitor might decline to claim, followed by one or two specific proof points. Ri's own example: staying flexible after the SOW is signed, which is where most engagements get rigid, proved by re-scoping mid-stream without a change order. Use that shape; use real facts from the sources.
- **Name the obstacle instead of selling past it.** If the work is hard, say why it is hard. Readers trust a plan that shows it knows where the difficulty is.

If the proof is not in the sources, write the claim with a visible placeholder ("[number of comparable implementations]") and add it to the open questions. Do not soften the claim into a generic one to avoid the gap.

## Ri's signature moves, as slide patterns

These are what make the copy sound like Ri rather than like any good writer. Look for a place to use them; do not force all of them into every deck.

1. **Name the reader's objection and answer it.** "Your finance team may worry that moving reports off Excel means losing the adjustments they make by hand. The new model keeps a governed adjustment step, so those adjustments are recorded instead of lost." On a slide: a callout, a "What you might be thinking" row, or an objection-and-answer pair.
2. **Concede, then redirect.** Give ground on what is true, then state what matters. The contrast pair is the spine: *what you don't need to do* / *what you need to do*. On a slide: two columns, or a title such as "You still own the vendor relationship, but you no longer manage the integration day to day."
3. **Argue by asymmetry.** Put the insight in the gap between two things: before and after, us and them, effort and value. "Your team spends 70% of close reconciling and 30% analyzing; we want to flip that ratio." Then-versus-now works especially well: "Two years ago, X wasn't possible; now Y is routine." Use a personal dated interval ("on a project 2.5 years ago…") only from a real anecdote.
4. **Ground and locate the abstraction.** Say where the idea lives, who uses it and in what situation. "Governance" becomes "the weekly change-review meeting where your architects approve schema changes". When an analogy or model explains a hard idea, keep the model intact and shorten its sentences instead.
5. **Enumerate the real mechanisms.** List the actual causes or steps instead of gesturing at them. "Rework comes from three places: missing member IDs, outdated provider addresses and duplicate submissions."
6. **Use three when there are three.** Ri likes the rule of three, when each item is distinct. Never invent a third item to fill a three-card layout; pick a different layout.
7. **Acknowledge, show action, then ask.** For a risk, delay or issue page: state the problem early and plainly, acknowledge its effect on the reader (including history, such as "this is the second date we have moved"), give the specific reason in a sentence or two, say what we are already doing, and give the next commitment with a date.
8. **End forward.** A closing page points to a concrete next step: a named meeting, a decision with a date, an owner. Never a summary of our capabilities.

## Visual structure

Ri likes slides that **can be dense but are easy to navigate**. Dense content is fine when the reader can find their way through it at a glance.

- **Bullets with short bolded lead-ins.** The most common pattern: a 2–4 word bolded title, then one sentence. A reader scanning only the bold text should get the gist.
- **Three bullet styles, chosen per content:**
  - **Title only.** A bolded phrase, when the point needs no elaboration.
  - **Short bullet.** No bold, 5–10 words, not necessarily a full sentence.
  - **Full bullet.** A **bolded title** followed by one full sentence.
- **A bullet is never more than two sentences.** Bullets are for scanning.
- **Bullets for parallel facts; numbers for decisions, asks or a real sequence.** Never number items that have no order.
- **Short paragraphs over long ones.** Two or three sentences at most in a text block.
- **Tables when rows and columns are real.** Ri is a big fan of tables for comparable items: options against criteria, controls against what they enable.
- **Visual first.** When content has relationships (a sequence, a flow, a hierarchy, a comparison, quantities), try a diagram, process flow, chart, roadmap or matrix before a text layout. Diagram labels must say what moves or who decides, not just name categories.
- **Layered disclosure.** The point and essential support in the main flow; deeper technical detail in a clearly referenced appendix or a "detail" panel.
- **Explore variations.** When the right form is not obvious, or the operator has not yet seen this kind of slide, build two to four variations with different templates or compositions (for example, one diagram-led and one text-dense) and let Ri choose. Record the choice as a preference for later slides in the deck.

## Anecdotes and personal experience

Personal anecdotes make a point concrete, and Ri welcomes them. They are claims about Ri's life, so:

1. **Use** a real anecdote found in the sources, memory or material in context.
2. **Ask** Ri for one, saying where it would go and what it should illustrate.
3. **Write without one** and note that a slot exists.

**Never invent an anecdote, a client story, a quotation or a personal "I remember when…".**

## Never write

- Aphorisms that trade something concrete for something abstract: "Steering is the work." "It's not just a file format; it's a philosophy."
- Setup lines that only announce what comes next.
- Inflated significance: "pivotal", "fundamental challenge", "game-changing".
- "Matters more than it looks" or "more than it may initially appear".
- Vague statements about our own effort or virtue: "working really hard", "fully committed", "we want to be transparent".
- Vague attribution: "experts agree", "research shows" without a source.
- Stacked hedges, or the same hedge phrase ("a reasonable case") used repeatedly.
- "That sort of thing" or any other hand-wave to end a list.
- "Proven track record". It is not banned, but Ri would not choose it; find the specific proof.

## Worked slide rewrites

These are examples of the voice applied to slide parts. Their facts are illustrative; never copy them into a deck as claims.

**Title**
- Not: "Data migration: the critical path to modernization"
- Not: "Migration is the risk." (aphorism)
- This: "Undocumented stored procedures, not data volume, will set the migration timeline"

**Card row → bolded-lead bullets**
- Not: three cards titled "Visibility", "Governance", "Acceleration", each promising to "unlock end-to-end value".
- This:
  - **Shared intake form.** Brokers submit once, so underwriters stop re-keying submissions from email.
  - **Rules-based triage.** Submissions under the small-commercial limit go straight to that queue; the rest get a senior review.
  - **Daily aging view.** Team leads see stuck submissions each morning instead of discovering them at month-end.

**Differentiator**
- Not: "Deep payer expertise and a proven track record of delivering results for organizations like yours."
- This: Title "We stay flexible after the SOW is signed, which is where most implementations get rigid." Bullets: "**Mid-stream re-scoping.** On [two recent payer implementations], we changed scope at the client's request without a change order." "**Named continuity.** The leads who scope the work stay on it through go-live."

**Risk or delay**
- Not: "Timeline update: unforeseen complexities have emerged"
- This: Title "Go-live moves from March 14 to April 4 because the vendor's API can't return claim history yet." Body: "This is the second date we have moved, and we know that makes your planning harder." "**Why.** The vendor's claim-history endpoint fails on records older than 2022; their fix is scheduled for March 21." "**What we're doing.** We are testing against a history extract in parallel, so the vendor fix is the only remaining dependency." "**Next.** We confirm April 4 or a fallback date by Friday, March 7."

**Comparison with an asymmetry**
- Not: a two-column "Current state / Future state" with "Manual processes" and "Automated, intelligent workflows".
- This: Title "Your analysts spend most of close reconciling, not analyzing; the new model reverses that." Columns show hours by activity today and after, with the gap called out.

**Process diagram labels**
- Not: boxes "Intake → Review → Approval" with unlabeled arrows.
- This: arrows labeled "complete submission", "flagged exceptions only", "approved scope over $250K", plus a one-line caption stating when the process advances.

**Next steps**
- Not: "Continue alignment on priorities and next steps."
- This (numbered because each needs a decision):
  1. **Choose pilot sites.** Your operations lead picks two plants by May 15.
  2. **Grant system access.** Your IT team opens read access to the MES by May 22.
  3. **Kick off baseline.** We start measuring on June 2.

## Out of scope for slides

The long-form profile includes traits that belong to email and messages, not to decks: greetings and sign-offs, the "->" connector, the frequent "really" of the appreciation register, and humor. Leave them out of slide copy unless Ri adds them.
