# Complexity benchmark for the modernization showcase

## Purpose and basis

The current showcase-v1 renders native, readable slides, but its most substantial pages still carry a small number of content relationships. This benchmark identifies what a follow-on showcase should exercise to demonstrate proposal-level composition across source-proposal patterns.

I inspected the rendered PNGs for UHG slides 8, 11, 12, 14, 24, 28, 36, 38, 43, and 44 in [`samples/reconstruction/reference/png`](../samples/reconstruction/reference/png), plus EnableComp slides 3, 4, 5, 27, and 33 in [`samples/catalog/render/enablecomp/png`](../samples/catalog/render/enablecomp/png) and [`samples/component-expansion/proposals/enablecomp`](../samples/component-expansion/proposals/enablecomp). I checked their XML-derived descriptions in [`samples/inspection/uhg/sidecars`](../samples/inspection/uhg/sidecars) and [`samples/inspection/enablecomp/sidecars`](../samples/inspection/enablecomp/sidecars). Both source decks are 16:9. UHG slide index and printed footer number agree for this sample; this should not be assumed for every source deck.

The counts below are evidence about the inspected files, not a score for slide quality. “Text chars” counts the extracted sidecar text body; it is affected by source run structure and extraction. “Text shapes” is not equivalent to unique narrative ideas: a table may be one native object with many cells, and an embedded architecture image may contain many visual nodes without corresponding slide shapes. Native screenshots, geometry, legibility, reading order, and the truthfulness of the content still require human review.

## Five representative patterns to benchmark

1. **Row-aligned current-to-future transformation with value logic** — [EnableComp 4](../samples/catalog/render/enablecomp/png/slide-004.png) and [33](../samples/component-expansion/proposals/enablecomp/slide-033.png). Use four or five workflow rows. Each row should connect a present condition, proposed change, human control or dependency, and the value measure that would test the change. Keep row correspondence visible across columns; braces/grouping, contrasting column headers, and a concise synthesis can clarify relationships. Reference 4 has five workflow rows and three main columns (current, future, value levers), with 32 shapes, 30 text shapes, three tables, and about 2,078 extracted characters. Reference 33 has four rows across three aligned columns, three native tables, and a concluding thesis. **This is the most useful harder replacement for showcase slide 3’s three numbered explanation cards.** The target is a relational matrix, not a larger stack of cards.

2. **Technical architecture with an argument for why each layer exists** — [UHG 14](../samples/reconstruction/reference/png/slide-014.png), supported by [11](../samples/reconstruction/reference/png/slide-011.png) and [12](../samples/reconstruction/reference/png/slide-012.png). Pair a substantial narrative column with a layered architecture or platform map: outcomes and users; information lifecycle; platform services; governance/security; workload placement; and the existing ecosystem. Explain how the layers connect and which controls cross-cut them. UHG 14 has only five text shapes and three pictures in its base slide inventory, yet the large architecture image is rich in grouped sections; its extracted narrative is about 1,121 characters across six bullets. This is a good example of why object counts alone understate visual complexity. UHG 11 pairs a four-item current-state case with a vision statement, product definition, four capability cards, and outcome line (one table; 9 text shapes). UHG 12 expresses five workstreams, each with two or three actions, then four “key risks” with icons (25 text shapes, 4 pictures). The benchmark should test the ability to preserve a visual model and its explanatory thread together.

3. **Phase scope page with activities, work products, evidence, assumptions, and options** — [UHG 24](../samples/reconstruction/reference/png/slide-024.png) and [28](../samples/reconstruction/reference/png/slide-028.png). Preserve the three-zone hierarchy: a strong phase/period panel with objective and exit criteria; a center Activities/Work Products matrix grouped by workstream; and a right evidence/assumptions/variable-scope zone. UHG 24/28 each contain a four-workstream, two-column activity/work-product matrix, several deliverable thumbnails, assumptions, and optional scope bullets. Sidecars record 15 shapes, 11 text shapes, five pictures, one table, and three groups on slide 24 (one group on 28), with roughly 1,250–1,310 extracted characters. The difficult part is alignment, not raw copy: retain row ownership and deliverable traceability across all three zones without turning every item into a detached card.

4. **Multi-phase plan that combines a calendar, work detail, outputs, and decision gates** — [UHG 38](../samples/reconstruction/reference/png/slide-038.png), [UHG 36](../samples/reconstruction/reference/png/slide-036.png), and [EnableComp 3](../samples/catalog/render/enablecomp/png/slide-003.png). EnableComp 3 shows five stages over a six-week engagement; each stage has an activity list and a distinct output block. UHG 38 maps a 9-month GA-readiness plan onto months 1–12 plus “Beyond,” with four staggered phase bars, milestone stars and captions, striped optional-extension tails, continuing management, and a legend. UHG 36 complements the calendar with two distinct five-step governance/intake pathways, an operating-principle sidebar, and a shared outcome band (34 shapes, 23 text shapes, two groups; about 1,263 extracted characters). Together these test both phase-column detail, parallel process logic, and scaled time geometry. EnableComp 3 has 30 shapes, 25 text shapes, and about 2,007 extracted characters; UHG 38 has 23 shapes, 14 text shapes, one table, and about 680 extracted characters. **This is the direct challenge for showcase slide 6:** four sparse period labels and four milestone boxes need to become explicit activities, outputs/exit criteria, dependencies, overlaps, and ownership along a legible time axis.

5. **Delivery organization, allocation, and capability roster** — [UHG 43](../samples/reconstruction/reference/png/slide-043.png) and [44](../samples/reconstruction/reference/png/slide-044.png). UHG 43 links leadership roles across WM and client sides, reporting lines, five shared specialist roles, three delivery pods, phase-only staffing, and a staffing legend; the right panel gives percent allocations and responsibilities. Its 41 shapes include 37 text shapes and four groups. UHG 44 complements the org chart with two roster sections: 10 core-team profiles and nine subject-matter experts, then responsibility summaries at right; its inventory includes 24 text shapes, 19 pictures, and two tables. **This is the direct challenge for showcase slide 8:** preserve the useful dynamic-pod skeleton, but show how workstream ownership, client counterparts, shared specialists, staffing allocation, phase scope, and transition fit together. If the narrative remains synthetic, use role profiles and explicitly synthetic allocations; do not imply real people or credentials.

## Additional high-density reference: proof with layered credibility

UHG [8](../samples/reconstruction/reference/png/slide-008.png) is a separate useful stress case for executive evidence. It combines two client case narratives, four project outcome figures, two survey metrics with a footnote, platform attribution, and four testimonial quotes with source roles/organizations. The slide contains 36 shapes, 26 text shapes, seven pictures, two groups, and about 2,980 extracted characters. Its visual hierarchy needs to let the proof scan before readers enter the paragraphs and quotations. This pattern is valuable after the five benchmarks above; it should not be approximated with unsourced or invented results in a synthetic showcase.

EnableComp [5](../samples/catalog/render/enablecomp/png/slide-005.png) is another compact but semantically dense argument page: five client needs feed into five evidence-backed response rows, each with an icon, bold claim, and supporting sentence. EnableComp [27](../samples/component-expansion/proposals/enablecomp/slide-027.png) uses a four-row deliverables table where each deliverable is paired with its contents and the decision it enables. These are good secondary checks for row alignment and proposal logic.

## Content and typography requirements for the next iteration

- Preserve a clear proposal arc: validated or clearly labeled current-state hypothesis → desired outcome → response and controls → activities and work products → phase gates and measures → delivery ownership and next decision. Each slide should make one decision or argument while carrying enough evidence to support it.
- Give table rows and lanes explicit relationships. Examples: current condition → change → human control → measure; activity → work product → approval gate; role → accountable capability → phase/allocation. A page should not reduce this to a collection of visually equal cards.
- Include supporting detail that changes decisions: ownership, data/control dependencies, assumptions, exit criteria, optional scope, baseline source, and next decision. Use specific nouns and verbs; remove generic purpose statements that do not specify evidence, work, or consequence.
- Use a typographic hierarchy, then measure it in PowerPoint. The inspected references have large headlines around 24–32 pt; typical table/copy runs around 10–12 pt; compact labels often around 8–10 pt; footnotes can be 7–9 pt. Direct-format XML observations span 7–32 pt on UHG 8, 8–24 pt on UHG 24/28, 10–18 pt on UHG 38, and 8–28 pt on EnableComp 3. Those ranges include headings and footnotes; they do **not** justify shrinking dense body copy to the minimum. Aim for 12–14 pt body copy where the layout allows, use 10–12 pt for genuinely dense cells, and keep footnotes/short labels subordinate. Split or redesign a page if essential copy will not remain readable. Several source runs inherit a theme font, so listed direct point sizes do not resolve all effective typography.
- Keep typeface, size, weight, color, margins, and usable width explicit in composition specs. Probe and use actual native PowerPoint measurements for each text block, including tables/cells and multiline paragraphs; do not infer fit from character counts. Render the final page and inspect at presentation size for crop, contrast, line spacing, collisions, and reading order.
- Keep all scenario data bounded and labeled. A hypothetical timeline, roles, allocations, baselines, metrics, and value levers are planning assumptions or examples, not client facts, commitments, or achieved outcomes. Client evidence and testimonials require their actual approved source and attribution.

## Evaluation approach and limits

Use the five patterns as acceptance fixtures for structural and narrative coverage, not as a single scalar density target. For each, inspect whether the page includes all required relationships, whether the text hierarchy survives at normal presentation size, and whether each row/lane/connector makes the intended logic easy to follow. Count text shapes, groups, pictures, cells, labels, and links only as diagnostic descriptors. A visually dense slide can still be confusing, repetitive, or inaccurate; a sparse slide can be excellent when its message warrants it. These source decks are examples for complexity review, not a blanket endorsement of their visual style or approval of every pattern as a reusable template.
