# West Monroe brand voice

This document is the working reference for writing as West Monroe. It collects the brand's messaging and voice guidance in one place, explains what each principle means in practice, and translates it into presentation copy. The `west-monroe-presentations` skill carries a condensed, agent-facing version of this material in `skills/west-monroe-presentations/references/brand-voice.md`. When the two disagree, update the skill from this file.

Sources: West Monroe Brand Compass, "Our Messaging" and "Our Brand Voice" (including the "Instead of / Try this" examples). Text quoted from those pages is marked as brand language. Everything else is interpretation for practical use and can be changed.

---

## 1. Who we are, in our own words

### Positioning

> Other consultancies talk at you.
> At West Monroe, we work with you.

The whole voice follows from this line. We are the partner at the table, not the one at the podium. Every piece of copy should sound like it was written by someone who is going to be in the room doing the work.

### Brand narrative (brand language)

> We're a global business and technology consulting firm passionate about creating measurable value for our clients, delivering real-world solutions.
>
> The combination of business and technology is not new, but how we bring them together is unique. We're fluent in both. We know that technology alone is not the answer, but how we apply it is. We rely on data to constantly adapt and solve new challenges. Actions that work today with outcomes that generate value for years to come.
>
> At West Monroe, we zero in on the heart of the opportunity, getting to results faster and preparing people for what's next.
>
> You'll feel the difference in how we work. We show up personally. We're right there in the room with you, co-creating through the challenges. With West Monroe, collaboration isn't a lofty promise, but a daily action. We work together with you to turn vision into clear action with lasting impact.

### Elevator pitch (brand language)

> At West Monroe, we work with you.
> We're a global consulting firm fluent in business and technology—building practical solutions with lasting impact. We work with clients to solve their most pressing issues to drive value through technology.

The brand guidance says to customize from there to your role and audience.

### Boilerplate (brand language)

> West Monroe is an AI-native global consulting firm built for today's pace of change. We combine deep industry expertise with modern technology and business transformation capabilities to help organizations move faster and deliver measurable impact with speed to value and certainty of results through execution excellence. We don't just talk about what's possible—we apply AI and technology where it matters most, pairing strategic insights with hands-on execution to help clients move from strategy to results, faster.
>
> Clients tell us we show up differently. Our employee ownership model fuels an all-in approach grounded in practical execution, measurable impact, and lasting partnership. That commitment has earned recognition from Forbes and Business Insider and made West Monroe one of only 22 companies recognized worldwide for building high-quality careers.

### How to use each one

| Material | Use it for | Do not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positioning line | The idea behind every page. On a slide, it can appear as written on a cover, closing or "about us" page. | Repeat it as a slogan on content pages. |
| Narrative | Background understanding of what we stand for. Read it, then write copy specific to the reader. | Paste it into a deck. It is conceptual reference, not slide copy. |
| Elevator pitch | A starting point for an introduction page or a speaker's opening, adapted to the audience and your role. | Use it unchanged on a page about a specific client problem. |
| Boilerplate | A formal company description where one is required, as written: RFP "about the firm" sections, a press-style "About West Monroe" page. | Edit its facts, paraphrase its recognition claims, or use pieces of it as proof on a client-specific page. Check that the recognition facts are current before a deck goes out. |
| Message anchors: "At West Monroe, we work with you." "Clear Action. Lasting Impact." "Own the now. Win the future." | Covers, section openers, closings. "Clear Action. Lasting Impact." is a logo/tagline lockup and should use the official artwork. | Use them as filler or repeat them on every asset. |

## 2. The character of our voice

The brand guidance describes three traits (brand language):

- **Passionately pragmatic**
- **Kindred creators**
- **Fiercely energized**

And the person behind them:

> You know that person you call when you really need help with something? Not the one who goes on and on about how it could be done. Not the one who makes endless promises about how great it will be when it's done. The one who knows how to do it. The one who gets it done.
>
> The one who is so passionate about what you're doing, they dive right in.

Key lines from the rest of the character description (brand language):

- "Co-creators in your success. Technology-smart and human-driven. Head and heart."
- "We take every business as seriously as our own, but we think solving problems is fun."
- "We never back down from a challenge. We always find a way."
- "Scrappy, roll-up-our-sleeves meets sharp intelligence and never-quit rigor."
- "We believe that digitally based solutions and insightful consulting mean nothing if they don't produce results."
- "We love working with our clients, transferring knowledge, building strength in their talent and resources."
- "Simply put, we're doers." / "More walk. Less talk."

### The essence: intelligence in action

> We speak honestly, clearly and directly with the confidence and enthusiasm of those who know what they're doing and enjoy doing it exceptionally well.

### What the traits mean when you write

| Trait | Sounds like | Does not sound like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passionately pragmatic | Naming the first thing we would do, who does it, and what changes. Practical detail stated with conviction. | Visionary language about transformation with no first step. Caution so heavy that nothing is recommended. |
| Kindred creators | "We" and "you" working on the same problem. Naming what the client's team brings and what we bring. Knowledge transfer as part of the plan. | "Our proprietary methodology delivers…" Copy where the client is only the recipient of our work. |
| Fiercely energized | Strong verbs, real numbers, a clear next step, visible interest in the problem. | Exclamation marks, superlatives, "exciting journey", hype about technology for its own sake. |

**Energy comes from substance.** The brand asks for enthusiasm, but enthusiasm in writing is created by specificity and momentum: a sharp observation about the reader's situation, a concrete plan, a number that matters, an invitation to start. It is not created by adjectives.

## 3. The four building blocks

Each block below has the brand's statement, its traits and techniques, the brand's own examples, and what it means for slides.

### 3.1 Start with the point

**Brand language:** "We're focused on what matters most to our audience, so don't bury the lede. Begin with what's most important and show that we understand what is valuable to them. Say what you mean, don't tell me what it means. Watch for jargon and meaningless, overused phrases. Data shows that simple word choices create more trust."

**Traits:** clear, pragmatic, focused, trustworthy, perceptive.
**Techniques:** tell it like it is; think audience-first; know your focus; choose clarity over abstraction.
**Brand examples:** "X% of businesses struggle with (y). Here's what we've learned…" / "We can do that. Here's where we'll start…"

**In practice**
- Decide what the reader most needs to take away before writing anything. Put it first: in the title of a slide, the first line of a section, the first sentence of a paragraph.
- "Say what you mean, don't tell me what it means" rules out copy that describes its own importance ("This is a critical insight") and copy that interprets instead of stating ("This represents a significant opportunity").
- Simple words beat impressive ones. "Use" not "leverage". "Start" not "commence". "Help" not "enable" where help is what you mean.

**On slides**
- The title states the conclusion as a sentence: "Claims rework comes from three intake errors, not adjudication." Not a topic: "Claims rework analysis".
- The first line of body copy supports the title directly. No scene-setting before the point.
- If a slide has no point you can state in one sentence, it is not ready to be written.

**Before / after (illustrative)**
- Before: "Leveraging advanced analytics to unlock supply chain visibility"
- After: "Your planners can see stockouts four days earlier if we connect the warehouse feed to the forecast"

### 3.2 Remember we're bilingual

**Brand language:** "We're fluent in both industry and technology. We're tech-forward and human-centered. Demonstrate that we know our stuff by connecting technology to how it powers successful outcomes. Don't overuse technical terms. Technology alone is not the purpose—how we apply it is. That's what's exciting to us. Our optimism for technology's possibilities comes through in our tone and messaging."

**Traits:** credible, bold, confident, smart, rigorous.
**Techniques:** balance tech and human; demonstrate knowledge; use real examples; bring a POV.
**Brand examples:** "Challenger brands need to be challenged too." / "X industry demands Y to succeed today…"

**In practice**
- Every technology mentioned should connect to what a person or the business can now do. "A shared data layer" alone is a component. "A shared data layer, so finance and operations close the month on the same numbers" is bilingual.
- Demonstrate knowledge with specifics only someone who has done the work would know: the step where these projects usually stall, the system that always causes trouble, the number from the last time we did it.
- Bring a point of view. A slide that presents three options without saying which one we would choose is not bilingual; it is a catalog.
- Use technical terms when the audience uses them, and explain them when it does not. Precision is not jargon. Jargon is a term used to sound expert rather than to be exact.

**On slides**
- Pair each technical element with its business effect: in the same bullet, the same row of a table, the same label of a diagram.
- An architecture or process slide should say what the structure makes possible, in the title or a callout.
- Use the client's industry vocabulary, not generic "organizations" language.

**Before / after (illustrative)**
- Before: "AI-powered intelligent document processing platform"
- After: "Extract policy data from scanned forms so underwriters stop retyping it"

### 3.3 Use "we" language

**Brand language:** "Our collaborative approach is a clear differentiator and an important part of who we are. Our voice should reflect the way we work and think. Keep the benefits at the forefront and include people in the conversation. Speak with people, not at them. Imagine you're at the same table, not a podium. Use connected, inclusive language that takes a 'we're in this together' approach. Use language that assures your audience that we can tackle the problem and they won't have to do it alone."

**Traits:** helpful, conversational, human, relatable, authentic.
**Techniques:** use a collaborative lens; use natural language; invite participation; stay grounded.
**Brand example:** "You're not alone. We've seen many businesses struggle with (x). We can show you how to solve this…"

**In practice**
- "We" means West Monroe together with the client when the work is genuinely shared. "You" addresses the reader directly. Avoid third-person distance ("the client", "the organization", "stakeholders") on client-facing pages.
- "We" language does not blur responsibilities. When ownership matters (a staffing plan, a RACI, a commercial page), say plainly who does what: "Your product owners set priorities each sprint; our team runs the build and testing."
- Natural language sounds like a capable person talking across a table. Read the copy aloud. If you would not say it to the client in a meeting, rewrite it.
- Inviting participation means asking for something specific: a decision, a working session, access to a system, a person to talk to.

**On slides**
- Use "your team", not "your people" or "resources".
- Show the client's role in the approach: who joins working sessions, who decides, what they learn along the way.
- Close sections and decks with a shared next step, not a summary of our capabilities.

**Before / after (illustrative)**
- Before: "The consultant will conduct stakeholder interviews and deliver a current-state assessment."
- After: "We'll interview your plant managers in week one and walk your team through what we find before we recommend anything."

### 3.4 Generate momentum

**Brand language:** "We inspire action and work confidently. Focus on value with a positive, active tone. Use quick, precise language that moves along. Grand generalizations are less meaningful. Demonstrate value with specifics and real-life stories. Show that we know how to get there and bring people with us."

**Traits:** action-oriented, connected, easy to read and understand.
**Techniques:** pace quickly; inspire action; use strong verbs and avoid the passive; be specific; edit words that don't contribute.
**Brand example:** "Let's do this. AI accelerates xyz… That's why we…"

**In practice**
- Use active voice with a named actor. "The data was migrated" hides who did it. "We migrated the data in six weeks" does not.
- Strong verbs carry energy: cut, replace, launch, consolidate, retire. Weak verbs drain it: enable, facilitate, support, drive (when nothing specific is being driven).
- Specifics move faster than generalizations. "Three weeks" beats "a short timeframe". "Two of your four regions" beats "selected areas".
- Real stories prove the point. A short, true example of what happened on a similar engagement does more than a claim of expertise. Never invent one.
- Every deck ends with a step the reader can take.

**On slides**
- Cut every word that does not carry meaning. Slides have no room for "in order to", "it is important to note that", "a wide range of".
- Sequence pages so each one moves the argument forward. A page that only restates the previous page stalls momentum.
- Put the next step on its own page or in a clear closing callout, with a date, an owner, or both.

**Before / after (illustrative)**
- Before: "Next steps will be determined following further discussion of priorities."
- After: "Pick two plants for the pilot by May 15 so we can start the baseline in June."

## 4. Instead of / Try this

The brand guidance includes three paired examples. They are the clearest statement of the voice, so they are reproduced here with notes on what changed.

| Instead of | Try this |
| --- | --- |
| Where forward thinking meets practical application. | Let's make smart thinking work for you |
| …partnering with companies in transformative industries to deliver quantifiable financial value. We believe that digital is a mindset... | If you're transforming your industry, you need a partner who works smarter to solve sooner and grow tangible value… |
| Learn everything West Monroe has to offer, from a supportive, collaborative culture to teammates who grow with you to outstanding benefits and flexible hybrid working | People come back to West Monroe. Why? Because when you put people first, supporting each other and co-creating a rewarding work environment is the natural outcome. Let's talk outstanding benefits, flexible working environment and super-smart people. |

**Why it works (brand language):** More personal. Direct. Leans into collaboration and practical application. Clearly said. Gets to the heart and turns the focus on them. More conversational. Human-centered. Emphasizes differentiation with a fact.

**What changed, pair by pair**

1. A tagline about us ("where forward thinking meets…") became an offer to the reader ("…work for **you**"). The abstract nouns (thinking, application) became a verb the reader benefits from (make it work).
2. Third-person description ("partnering with companies") became second-person address ("If **you're** transforming…"). The belief statement ("digital is a mindset") disappeared because it told the reader what we think rather than what they get.
3. A list of perks became a claim backed by a fact ("People come back to West Monroe"), followed by the reason. The fact is the differentiator; the perks come after it.

**The pattern to reuse:** turn the focus onto the reader, replace an abstract claim with an observable one, and lead with the fact that sets us apart.

## 5. How the voice flexes

The voice stays the same. How much of each building block you use changes with the situation.

| Situation | Lean toward | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Executive proposal or pursuit | Start with the point; a sharp point of view; proof attached to every differentiator | Generic differentiators any competitor could claim |
| Technical audience (architects, engineering leads) | Bilingual precision; exact terms; mechanisms | Glossing over technical facts to sound friendly; buzzwords that technical readers distrust |
| Working session or discovery | "We" language; questions; invitation to participate | Presenting conclusions before the client has contributed |
| Status, risk or bad news | Start with the point; honesty; what we are doing about it | Burying the issue; vague reassurance; self-praise about effort |
| Internal (WM-only) deck | Directness; candor about risks and win logic | Leaking internal win logic or candid commentary into client-facing pages |
| Introduction or perspective piece | A point of view; a real example; momentum toward a conversation | Capability lists; boilerplate on every page |

## 6. Words and phrases

### Prefer

- Plain verbs: use, build, fix, cut, replace, start, decide, move, test, launch, retire.
- The reader's own terms: their system names, their roles, their metrics, their industry vocabulary.
- Concrete quantities with units and a basis: "18% fewer manual touches in the pilot region".
- "Your team", "we", "you".

### Avoid

| Avoid | Why | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| leverage, synergy, utilize, holistic, robust, seamless, best-in-class, world-class, cutting-edge, next-generation | Words used to sound impressive; they tell the reader nothing | Say what the thing does |
| unlock, empower, enable, drive (with no object that changes) | Gesture at value without naming it | Name the outcome and who gets it |
| transformation, journey, ecosystem, paradigm, digital mindset (as standalone ideas) | Grand generalizations the brand guidance asks us to avoid | Name the specific change |
| "deep expertise", "proven track record", "trusted partner", "commitment to your success", "relentless focus on outcomes", "uniquely positioned" | Every consulting firm says these | Name the specific experience, number or behavior that proves it |
| "It is important to note", "In today's rapidly changing landscape", "As we all know" | Throat-clearing | Delete and start with the point |
| Passive constructions ("will be delivered", "was identified") | Hide the actor and slow the pace | Name who does it |

Use standard American English and the Oxford comma. The West Monroe seller skills avoid em dashes in client-facing copy; in slides, prefer a colon, a period or a second line.

## 7. Writing presentation copy in the brand voice

Slides compress everything. The brand voice still applies, but each element has its own job.

| Element | Job | Brand-voice guidance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cover title | Name the reader's opportunity or decision | About them, not about us. "Getting claims to adjudication in two days" beats "West Monroe claims transformation proposal". |
| Eyebrow / section label | Orient the reader | Short and literal: "Approach", "Week 1–4", "What we heard". Not a slogan. |
| Slide title | State the point of the page | A full assertion the reader could agree or disagree with. Usually one line, at most two. |
| Subtitle / lead-in | Add the one qualification or "so what" the title cannot carry | Optional. Never a restatement of the title. |
| Card or column titles | Name the distinct ideas | Specific nouns or short verb phrases. If the three titles could appear in any proposal, rewrite them. |
| Body copy and bullets | Support the title | Concrete mechanisms, actors and numbers. Active voice. Parallel structure. |
| Labels in diagrams | Explain relationships | Arrows and boxes should say what moves or who decides, not just category names. |
| Callouts and big numbers | Make one fact unmissable | Always with its basis and source. A number without context is decoration. |
| Source and footnotes | Earn trust | Plain and complete. Mark estimates and illustrative values as such. |
| Closing / next steps | Generate momentum | A shared next action with a time or an owner. "Let's" is welcome when a real action follows it. |
| About West Monroe | Formal description | Use the boilerplate or elevator pitch as written, plus proof specific to this client. |

## 8. Brand voice and Ri Scott's voice

The `west-monroe-presentations` skill applies Ri's personal slide voice by default, on top of this brand voice. Both are described in the skill (`references/ri-slide-voice.md`); the full long-form profile is `docs/skill-planning/ri.md`. They agree on most things: start with what matters, be specific, prefer concrete mechanisms to abstractions, attach proof to claims, sound like a capable partner.

Where they pull in different directions, the skill resolves it like this:

| Tension | Resolution |
| --- | --- |
| The brand's "pace quickly" and "Let's do this" energy vs. Ri's dislike of punchy, slogan-like lines | Energy comes from strong verbs, real numbers and a concrete next step. Short is fine; slogan is not. A compressed line must still name an actor, a mechanism or a number. |
| "Start with the point" vs. Ri building to the outcome in explanatory writing | The slide title always states the point. The body may build toward it when it is explaining how something works. |
| "We" language vs. Ri's precise ownership | Use "we" for genuinely shared work and name owners when responsibility matters. |
| Brand optimism about technology vs. Ri's habit of naming the reader's objection | Name the objection, answer it with a mechanism, then show what becomes possible. Optimism that has dealt with the doubt is more credible. |

## 9. Checklist

Before copy goes to review, check:

1. **The point.** Can you state each page's point in one sentence, and is that sentence the title?
2. **Their focus.** Is the copy about the reader's situation and outcome more than about West Monroe?
3. **Bilingual.** Does every technical element connect to a business or human effect?
4. **Point of view.** Does the page recommend something, or only describe options?
5. **Proof.** Is each differentiator or benefit backed by a specific fact, number or real example, with its source?
6. **"We" and "you".** Is the language direct and collaborative, with ownership clear where it matters?
7. **Momentum.** Active voice, strong verbs, no throat-clearing, and a clear next step at the end.
8. **The competitor test.** Could another consulting firm put its name on this sentence unchanged? If so, rewrite it.
