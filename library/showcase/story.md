# Software modernization showcase story

**Illustrative proposal scenario — synthetic.** This narrative demonstrates varied slide structures, shapes, and local assets for a hypothetical regional service company consolidating customer and operations systems. The organization, operating model, stakeholders, scope, schedule, and all example data are invented for design demonstration. They are not client facts or claimed results. Numeric values below are proposal planning assumptions only, not measured outcomes.

## Narrative arc

The proposal moves from the decision at hand, through the reason to act and the proposed operating model, into a bounded delivery plan and governance. Each page has one job and a clear reading order.

### 1. Cover — Make the core platforms easier to change

**Role:** Establish the proposal subject and scenario immediately.

**Takeaway:** A sequenced modernization can reduce platform friction while preserving service continuity.

**Content:** Proposal for a hypothetical regional service company; “Illustrative scenario — synthetic”; September 2026. Use the abstract highlighted-glass-cube photograph as a full-height/cropped visual with restrained headline treatment. Place the official logo on a light field with clear space.

### 2. Decision page — Fund discovery before committing to a target design

**Role:** Give the executive sponsor a concrete ask.

**Takeaway:** Approve a short, evidence-gathering discovery to confirm priorities, constraints, and investment choices.

**Content:** Three decision cards: sponsor a cross-functional discovery; provide access to application, integration, and service evidence; nominate owners for architecture and operations decisions. Close with the decision needed: authorize discovery planning, with scope and fee to be agreed after stakeholder alignment. No savings or payback claims.

### 3. Current-state case — Change is slowed at the seams

**Role:** Explain the problem without pretending to know a client baseline.

**Takeaway:** The illustrative challenge is the coordination cost between systems, data, and teams.

**Content:** Show a simple current-state chain of customer request → case platform → integration layer → operations tools, with manual reconciliation and duplicated rules as *scenario hypotheses to validate*. A sidebar lists discovery evidence needed: interface inventory, change lead times, incident themes, duplicate data definitions. Label all as hypotheses, not findings.

### 4. Desired outcomes — Design for four observable capabilities

**Role:** Turn an abstract modernization into testable outcomes.

**Takeaway:** Define success in terms of capabilities before choosing technologies.

**Content:** Four N2 cards: consistent customer and case data; independently deployable services; visible integration health; controlled release and recovery. Each card has a “how to verify” line (e.g., agreed data ownership map, deployment boundary, service-level telemetry, rehearsed rollback). Use the hosted database/cloud icons as modest line-art cues.

### 5. Solution — Modernize around stable interfaces

**Role:** Show the target concept and how its parts relate.

**Takeaway:** Separate experience, domain services, integration, and data responsibilities behind managed interfaces.

**Content:** Layered architecture diagram: channels; customer and case capabilities; API/event integration; governed data products; cloud/runtime foundation. Connect layers with native arrows and annotate security, observability, and identity as cross-cutting controls. Mark this as a conceptual target architecture, subject to discovery and platform constraints. Use the connecting-arrow accent once as an editorial pointer.

### 6. Delivery team — Small pods align work to outcomes

**Role:** Make the delivery model tangible.

**Takeaway:** Stable cross-functional pods own bounded capabilities, with shared architecture and change support.

**Content:** One platform/enabling pod supports two outcome pods: Customer & Case; Integration & Data. Each pod card names product lead, architecture, engineering, QA, and operations participation; do not assign real people or imply fixed staffing. A thin governance band shows sponsor decisions, architecture review, and business-owner validation. Use dotted/dashed arrow only for an explicitly optional feedback loop.

### 7. Workplan — Learn, prove, then scale

**Role:** Show a credible sequence and decision gates.

**Takeaway:** Commit in stages, and let evidence from each stage shape the next.

**Content:** Four phases shown as a timeline: (1) Discover & align, (2) Prove one vertical slice, (3) Expand priority capabilities, (4) Stabilize & transition. Illustrative planning ranges: 3–4 weeks, 6–8 weeks, 10–16 weeks, 3–4 weeks respectively; ranges are placeholders to validate with scope, procurement, and team availability. Gates: target decisions; end-to-end slice accepted; release readiness; operations handoff. Label schedule “illustrative planning range.”

### 8. Measures — Establish the baseline before setting targets

**Role:** Demonstrate measurement discipline.

**Takeaway:** Agree operational baselines and target thresholds in discovery; do not forecast benefits from invented inputs.

**Content:** Example measure table with metric, baseline source, owner, and target-setting point: lead time for change (delivery telemetry; engineering); failed change rate (release records; operations); interface incident volume (incident system; service owner); duplicate customer records (data profiling; data owner). Include a small blank/“baseline pending” scorecard instead of fake chart values. This page can use table and metric shapes without outcome claims.

### 9. Governance — Surface decisions before they become delays

**Role:** Clarify how decisions, dependencies, and risk will be managed.

**Takeaway:** A weekly decision rhythm and explicit dependency owners keep the work sequenced.

**Content:** Three-column operating cadence: pod review (weekly delivery blockers), architecture forum (design exceptions and standards), steering (scope, risk, investment). Risk/control matrix: undocumented interfaces → inventory and owner; unclear data authority → domain decision; cutover risk → rehearsed rollback and staged traffic. Present as anticipated risks for the hypothetical scenario.

### 10. Team — Bring business and technology owners into the same room

**Role:** Show the human collaboration model.

**Takeaway:** Progress depends on named decision owners and day-to-day participation from operations.

**Content:** Four role profiles rather than fabricated biographies: executive sponsor (priorities and funding); product owner (workflow acceptance); platform architect (guardrails and exceptions); operations lead (service readiness). Use role silhouettes or simple native shapes; do not use stock portraits or made-up named biographies. Add a callout that discovery confirms role availability and decision rights.

### 11. Next step — Align the people, evidence, and first decision

**Role:** End with a specific, low-regret action.

**Takeaway:** A working session can test the scenario and shape a bounded discovery brief.

**Content:** Proposed 90-minute working session agenda: confirm business outcomes; map critical journeys and systems; review evidence owners and access; agree discovery participants and decision path. Ask the sponsor to nominate business, technology, and operations counterparts. Close with “Illustrative scenario — synthetic.” Use the hand-drawn underscore on one short phrase; use the yellow highlight only behind 1–4 words in a headline on a white field.
